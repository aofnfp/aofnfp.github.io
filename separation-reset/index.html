The Camera Footage Ruined Your Day Again.

You set up the camera because you hoped you'd been imagining it. You weren't. Twenty minutes after you left, the barking started. Then the door frame. You watched your dog — your sweet, gentle dog — claw at the door like something was chasing them. Something was: panic.

If that's your dog every time you leave — the barking, the destruction, the accidents, the escape attempts — this page is for you.

It's Not Disobedience. It's Panic.

Here's the reframe that changes everything: your dog isn't being stubborn, spiteful, or "testing you." Dogs with separation anxiety are experiencing a genuine panic disorder. They learn your departure cues — keys, shoes, coat — the way you learned to flinch at a dentist's drill, and the fear starts before you even reach the door.

That's why scolding never works. You can't punish a panic attack out of a dog any more than you could punish one out of a person.

"The fear starts before you reach the door. The dog who shreds the doorframe isn't angry at you — they're trying to get back to the only thing that makes the panic stop."

Everything in the Reset follows from that one insight: keep every step under the panic threshold, and the panic has nothing to feed on.

Why the Usual Advice Makes It Worse

Crate them — but crating a panicking dog causes documented injuries: broken teeth, torn nails, bloody paws. Let them cry it out — but flooding a panicked dog sensitizes them, it doesn't desensitize them. Get a second dog — but a second dog doesn't fix separation distress, it just gives you two dogs. Long, emotional goodbyes — but those just load more anxiety onto the departure.

The Reset replaces all of it with one written plan: how to manage your dog while you train, how to defuse departure cues until they're boring, and a graduated absence protocol in seconds — not minutes — with word-for-word calm departure and return scripts.

Let's be clear about what that means here. You will never punish, startle, or "correct" your dog for panicking. You will never use a shock, prong, or choke collar. That's how desensitization actually works.

Questions

Is this a cure for separation anxiety?

No — and anyone who promises you a 14-day cure is selling something else. This is a 14-day foundation: the assessment, the cue work, the graduated protocol, and the tracking habit that real recovery is built on. The guide is honest that severe cases take weeks or months.

My dog's case seems severe. Should I still start?

Start with Chapter 1's red-flag gate. If your dog self-injures, breaks out of crates, refuses food, or panics before you even leave, the guide tells you to see your vet (or a certified behaviorist — DACVB, CAAB, or CSAT) before starting the protocol. Medication is framed as your vet's decision, never yours.

We crate our dog. Does this judge that?

No — it tells you the truth about it: crating a dog that panics can cause serious injuries, and most separation-anxiety cases do worse crated. The rule inside is simple: crate only if your dog is already calm in it, and stop at the first escape attempt.

How is this different from free training tips online?

Free tips contradict each other — crate them, don't crate them, ignore them, comfort them. This is one coherent system built on published behavior protocols (the sources page cites every one), with a day-by-day plan and a tracker that tells you exactly when to advance.

What if it doesn't work for us?

Then the 30-day guarantee above applies. And the troubleshooting chapter is honest about next steps: pause, call your vet, and consider professional help — not "try harder."
